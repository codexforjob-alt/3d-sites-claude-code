# Page structures

Pick the structure from the subject, not from habit. The temptation is to reach for the same
shape every time — full-bleed hero, pinned manifesto, horizontal case reel, closing CTA —
because it works. It does work, and it is also instantly recognisable as a formula once you
have seen it twice.

Each structure below implies a different plate manifest, so choosing one is also choosing
what to ask Veo for. Adapt freely; the point is to make a decision rather than default.

## A · The reel

Full-bleed hero → pinned statement the text scrubs over → horizontal card reel → wipe →
people/place → full-bleed CTA.

**Suits:** studios, agencies, production, architecture practices, anyone whose portfolio *is*
the pitch.
**Plates:** hero loop, one pinned plate, one vertical per case, a transition, a portrait, a
closing plate.
**Watch for:** horizontal pinning is hostile on touch — always specify the stacked fallback.
**Register:** usually dark, but a photography studio shooting daylight interiors should not
be.

## B · The specimen

One object held in the centre for the whole page. Sections change what you know about it
rather than what you are looking at: the plate stays, the copy and the lighting change around
it. Scroll drives the object's rotation or a lighting shift.

**Suits:** a single product, a watch, a bottle, a car, a piece of software with one core idea.
**Plates:** four to six angles or lighting states of the same subject, ideally shot to cut
together; one macro detail; one context shot.
**Watch for:** this lives or dies on the plates matching each other. Tell the user explicitly
that these six prompts must share camera, lens, and grade language word-for-word, or the cuts
will not sit together.

## C · The room

Continuous forward movement through a space, sections attached to what you pass. Feels like
one unbroken take even though it is six clips.

**Suits:** restaurants, hotels, clinics, gyms, galleries, property — anywhere the thing being
sold is a place.
**Plates:** a sequence of moves through the space in a consistent direction, plus two or three
macro details to cut away to.
**Watch for:** direction of travel must be consistent across clips or the illusion collapses.
Put "camera moves forward at constant speed" in every prompt.

## D · The ledger

A dense, information-first page — comparison tables, specs, pricing, numbers — with plates
used sparingly as punctuation between blocks rather than as backdrop.

**Suits:** B2B, technical products, services where the buyer is comparing, anything with real
numbers to show.
**Plates:** three or four only. Restraint is the point; video here is a breath between dense
sections, not the substance.
**Watch for:** do not put text over video in this structure. The content is the content.

## E · The index

A grid or list that is the whole page — many items, each with its own plate, filterable or
sortable. No hero in the usual sense; the first screen is already the content.

**Suits:** menus, catalogues, large portfolios, property listings, event line-ups.
**Plates:** many short loops, one per item, all shot to the same recipe so the grid reads as
a set.
**Watch for:** the plate count gets expensive fast. Tell the user the per-item cost up front
and suggest starting with six items and posters for the rest.

## F · The letter

Long-form, typographically led, mostly text. One plate opens it, one closes it, perhaps one
in the middle. The reading experience is the design.

**Suits:** manifestos, founder stories, editorial, a single strong argument, non-profits.
**Plates:** two or three, but they carry enormous weight — each one has to be the best clip
in the set.
**Watch for:** this is the hardest to make feel expensive, because there is nowhere to hide.
The typography has to be genuinely good.

## Choosing

Ask what the visitor most needs to understand in the first ten seconds.

- What the work looks like → **A**
- What the object is → **B**
- What the place feels like → **C**
- Whether the numbers work → **D**
- How much choice there is → **E**
- Why any of it matters → **F**

Mixing two is fine when it is deliberate — a room opening onto a ledger is a good hotel page.
Mixing three usually means the decision has not been made.
